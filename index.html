What I bring

Five threads that run through the work, and where to see them.

Data quality first

Before any analysis I check what the data can and cannot support: duplicates, incomplete records, how each row was collected, and whether a measure means what it claims. And I know when a "problem" isn't one.

See: 871 duplicate loan records; same-day split baskets inflating retention; incomplete recent complaint weeks; a failed randomization check; map data undercounting cafés; 6,562 identical rows in the email test that were real customers, so I kept them.

Consumer behavior

Why customers leave, complain, respond to a campaign, or lose money to a scam. I read behavior from reviews, complaints, experiments and transactions.

See: Wombloom, bank app reviews, Olist, email test, Cookie Cats

Fast, practical solution design

When the obvious route is blocked, I design a workable one quickly and write it down: a different data source, a better measure, or a simple rule a team can run every week.

See: switching to app reviews when complaint text was unavailable; extracting fraud tables from PDFs; an alert rule that doesn't go blind after a spike; a break-even model teams fill with their own numbers; a one-day field test as the go/no-go.

Analytics that ends in a decision

A finding isn't finished until it has a price, a threshold or a recommendation, plus the assumptions it depends on.

See: loan cut-off, email profit, friction break-even, café location

Visualization that makes the point

Charts that state their takeaway, show uncertainty, and use a palette I tested for color-blind readers.

See the "How I showed it" note under every chart.

How I think

  • Start from the decision. Who has to act, and what would change their mind? My recent projects begin with a written plan and predictions logged before I see the results.
  • Distrust the data first. Most wrong answers come from how data was collected, not from the model.
  • Real is not the same as worth acting on. A significant effect still needs a cost, a margin and a break-even point.
  • Say where it breaks. Every case study ends with a reflection: what I got wrong or where the answer is weak, and what I learned.

Built from scratch: data I collected or assembled myself

No ready-made dataset existed for these questions, so I built one, then checked it before trusting it.

What should my pregnancy-journal app build first?

The problemI designed Wombloom, a private pregnancy journal for iPhone. Before deciding what to build first, I wanted evidence beyond my own experience: what do parents praise and complain about in the apps they already use?

The data7,955 App Store reviews of 10 competing apps, collected through Apple's public feeds with a documented method for choosing competitors. Reviewer names and IDs were never stored.

What I caughtAn off-the-shelf sentiment model called only 44% of 1 and 2 star reviews negative, so I built topic rules and tested them on held-out reviews. Only 7 of 19 topics passed, and only those count as measurements.

What I found
  • Trust problems cost the most. Reviews that mention ads, updates or bugs average 2.4 to 2.6 stars, against 4.16 overall.
  • Pricing is the most discussed pain point: 20% of reviews mention it, and 36.5% of those are 1 or 2 stars.
  • Journaling is the clearest opening, but a thin one: reviews of journal-style apps talk about memories about 9 times as often, mostly for two apps.
Interactive dashboardCode and case study

Where I was wrong

I expectedMy first version of the app to cover what parents need most.

The data showedIt held up on trust (no ads, no account, no data collected) but entries live only on the phone, and complaints about lost data point to backup and export.

What I changedBackup and export moved to the top of my build list.

Challenge and lesson

ChallengeThe priority model ranked my own favourite idea low.

LessonI didn't tune the model until it agreed. I kept the disagreement in the write-up and added a second lens, because a reader should see the tension too.

What should a bank fix first in its mobile app?

The problemBank apps collect thousands of reviews. A digital product team needs to know which problems actually cost ratings, and whether fintech users complain about different things than bank customers.

The data8,350 recent reviews of 17 bank, card and fintech apps that I collected from Apple's public review feed.

What I caughtAverage rating measured how often each app prompts happy users, not its quality (0.99 correlation with its five-star share), so I compared only unhappy reviews.

What I found
  • Unhappy users write about the app working: 37% of 1 and 2 star bank reviews mention login or bugs.
  • Common is not the same as costly. Redesigns are the most talked-about theme but the weakest link to a low rating; frozen or closed accounts are rare at banks (3%) but the costliest (odds ratio 12.8).
  • Fintech users have a different problem: they mention frozen accounts 2.7 times and fraud 3.5 times as often as bank customers.
Code and case studyResearch plan

Where I was wrong

I expectedCustomer service to be the theme that costs the most stars.

The data showedIt ranked fifth. Frozen or closed accounts cost the most, though they are rarely mentioned.

What I changedI now measure how often a problem comes up and how much it costs as two separate questions.

Challenge and lesson

ChallengeThe complaint narratives I planned to use were unavailable, and my first keyword rules scored precision as low as 0.47 on a labeled validation sample.

LessonChange the route, not the question: I switched to app reviews, rewrote the rules from their errors, and scored them once on a fresh sample before trusting them.

Where should a café brand open next in Hanoi?

The problemA café brand wants to know which district has the most unmet demand at a sensible rent. No dataset answers that.

The dataI built one: 15,484 points of interest from OpenStreetMap, land prices scraped from a property listings site, and population from a pinned Wikipedia revision.

What I caughtOpenStreetMap appears to miss small independent cafés outside the centre, so every supply gap is re-estimated on chain cafés only.

What I found
  • The market is concentrated: Hoàn Kiếm has 2% of residents but 28% of mapped cafés.
  • The biggest gap is Thanh Xuân: 31 mapped cafés where its demand predicts about 123, on land about 20% cheaper.
  • The answer is robust: first in 80% of 10,000 random weightings, top three in 90%.
Code and case study

Where it breaks

The weak pointThanh Xuân's lead depends on the supply-gap signal. Drop it, and Đống Đa ranks first.

What I didI said so up front and recommended a one-day field count of the shortlisted blocks as the go/no-go test.

Challenge and lesson

ChallengeHanoi had abolished its districts in July 2025, while land prices and population were still published by the old districts.

LessonI queried the historical map and pinned every source to a date, so the analysis can be rebuilt exactly.

Where do scam losses concentrate, and where should a bank add friction?

The problemEvery check a bank adds to a payment annoys honest customers. A fraud team needs to know where friction prevents enough losses to be worth it.

The dataThe Federal Trade Commission publishes its fraud figures only as PDF reports, so I extracted six years of tables (2019 to 2024) and combined them with 31,959 scam complaints filed with the CFPB and Census population data.

What I caughtMy first trend chart put two measures on two y-axes, which invents a relationship, so I split it into three small charts.

What I found
  • A few large losses are most of the money: 12.6% of loss reports hold at least 87.6% of the dollars, while the median loss is $497.
  • Bank transfers and crypto dominate: about $44,000 lost per bank-transfer report, 17 times a credit-card report.
  • Who bears the loss decides everything: only 6.3% of scam complaints end in a refund, and if banks reimbursed, friction would pay off at a scam rate 16 times lower.
Code and case studyResearch plan

Where I was wrong

I expectedFriction on bank transfers to break even only above a 1% scam rate, given how rarely banks refund today.

The data showedAt my base assumptions it breaks even at 0.72%, so friction pays off sooner than I thought.

What I changedI report the break-even across a range of costs and effectiveness, so a fraud team can plug in its own numbers.

Challenge and lesson

ChallengeThe data existed only as PDFs, and the payment-method tables give totals but no medians.

LessonI extracted the tables and checked them against the PDF text, and where a median was missing I used loss per report and said so, rather than inventing precision.

Customer behavior and experiments

Does a marketing email change what customers do, and is it worth the cost?

The problemA retailer tested two emails on 64,000 customers. Marketing needs to know which to send, if either.

Live appCode and case study
What I found
  • Customers respond: the Men's email lifts visits from 10.6% to 18.3%.
  • Real is not worth it by default: the Men's email earns about $0.26 per customer after costs; the Women's ($0.12) barely clears zero.
  • Checking results daily declares a false winner 21% of the time, not 5%.

Where I was wrong

I expectedAdjusting for customers' past spend to sharpen the estimates.

The data showedIt removed 0.04% of the noise, because past spend barely predicts a rare two-week purchase.

What I changedI check whether a technique's assumption holds before relying on it, and report it either way.

Challenge and lesson

ChallengeBoth emails had statistically real effects, which made both tempting to recommend.

LessonA result is not a decision until it has a price: after margin and sending cost, only one email clearly pays.

Does moving a game's paywall gate later keep more players?

The problemA mobile game makes players wait or pay at a "gate". The team tested moving it from level 30 to level 40.

Code and case study
What I found
  • Most players leave before any gate: only about a third reach level 30.
  • Moving it hurts: 7-day retention falls 0.8 points; keep the gate at 30.
  • The result is solid even though the test had a flaw (below).

Where I was wrong

I expectedThe experiment's random split to be clean.

The data showedIt failed its sample-ratio check: 789 more players in one group.

What I changedI re-ran the result under the worst case for that imbalance (still 0.66 points lower) and flagged the cause for the team to find.

Challenge and lesson

ChallengeOnly about a third of players ever reach the gate, so its effect is diluted across everyone in the test.

LessonCheck the experiment before reading its result, and say who the effect actually applies to.

Why don't marketplace customers come back?

The problemOlist, a Brazilian marketplace, sees few repeat buyers. Is late delivery the reason, and what should it fix first?

Code and case study
What I found
  • True retention is about 2%, not 3%: 30% of "repeat" orders were split baskets on the same day.
  • Lateness drives bad reviews: from about 10% negative on time to about 80% at 8 or more days late.
  • Fixing delivery protects reviews, not loyalty. Start with the Rio de Janeiro route (42% of excess late orders).

Challenge and lesson

ChallengeSplit baskets made customers look loyal, and a standard RFM segmentation quietly split identical customers into different segments.

LessonBefore segmenting or counting repeat customers, I check what the data's structure does to the measure.

Risk and monitoring in banking

Which loan applicants should a lender approve?

The problemA bad loan loses far more than a good loan earns, so the approval cut-off is a cost decision, not an accuracy contest.

What I caughtCounting young loans as "good" made recent loans look risk-free, so only loans with a full 12-month window are used.

Code and case study
What I found
  • An explainable scorecard beats the platform's own score (AUC 0.711 vs 0.647) on loans it had never seen.
  • Approving the safest 76% earns $0.39M more than approving everyone, on $104M of loans.
  • The margin assumption matters more than the model: the best approval rate ranges from 30% to 96%.

Where I was wrong

I expectedThe lending platform's own score to be about as good as anything I built.

The data showedMy scorecard ranked risk better, by 0.064 AUC.

What I changedI treat an existing model as a benchmark to test, not a ceiling to assume.

Challenge and lesson

ChallengeA boosted model scored slightly higher, and a version given post-loan fields scored 0.99 by reading the outcome.

LessonAsk what each field could have known at decision time, and prefer the explainable model when the extra accuracy isn't real.

Which customer complaints need attention this week?

The problemA complaint team can review only a handful of issues a week. Which surges deserve it, and can the alert rule be trusted?

Code and case study
What I found
  • A robust alert rule flags about 6 new issues a week and reliably catches surges in larger streams.
  • The textbook rule went blind for 26 weeks after one huge complaint wave.
  • 70% of alerts are short bursts, so triage should separate waves from lasting problems.

Where I was wrong

I expectedA robust alert rule to shrug off one huge spike.

The data showedIt was partly blinded for months, needing 3 to 5 times the new normal to alert.

What I changedI built an alert-aware baseline that caps flagged weeks, and it held up.

Challenge and lesson

ChallengeThe newest two weeks in the data were incomplete, and 114 of 250 complaint streams were too small to monitor on their own.

LessonMonitor only complete data, and roll small streams up rather than pretend they can be watched individually.

The big picture

Nine projects across banking, retail, consumer apps and a café chain, and one recurring lesson: the number people look at first is rarely the one that decides the outcome.

Rare, severe experiences drive outcomes

Frozen accounts appear in 3% of unhappy bank reviews but cost the most stars. 12.6% of scam loss reports hold most of the money lost. A late delivery takes negative reviews from about 10% to about 80%.

So: watch the tail, not the average.

Trust is the product

Ads, updates and bugs cost pregnancy apps the most stars. Login and bugs dominate unhappy bank reviews. Fintech users write about frozen accounts and fraud.

So: reliability and fair treatment come before new features.

Averages and defaults mislead

Average app ratings measured how often apps ask for them. "Repeat" customers were split baskets. Recent loans looked safe only because they were young. The typical scam loss is $497; the average is 25 times that.

So: ask how a number was produced before reading it.

What this means for a team: bring me a decision and messy, incomplete data. I'll find or build the data it needs, check it before trusting it, put a price on the answer, and tell you plainly where it could be wrong.

Methods and tools

Data collection

Public APIs and feeds, polite web scraping, PDF table extraction, OpenStreetMap queries, privacy rules at ingestion.

Data quality

Integrity and completeness checks, deduplication, identity resolution, sample-ratio checks, validation against labeled samples, documented known gaps.

Consumer and text analysis

Review mining, topic rules validated on held-out labels, retention and survival analysis, segmentation.

Inference and experiments

A/B tests, sample-ratio checks, bootstrap intervals, multiple-comparison control, power and peeking.

Modeling and decisions

Logistic and count regression, scorecards, gradient boosting, calibration, break-even and sensitivity analysis.

Tools and visualization

Python (pandas, statsmodels, scikit-learn), SQL (DuckDB, SQLite), Jupyter, Git; matplotlib, Altair and Streamlit apps with color-blind-tested palettes.
